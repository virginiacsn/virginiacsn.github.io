## TL;DR

- **Question:** wearables collect physiological data passively, but can they identify
  menstrual cycle phase, and how do they compare with hormone assays and daily symptom
  diaries, which cost more to collect?
- **Approach:** seven combinations of Fitbit wearable data, hormone
  measurements, and daily self-reports for classifying cycle phase (Menstrual,
  Follicular, Fertility, Luteal), using 42 participants (3,694 participant-days) from a
  public PhysioNet dataset.
- **Wearables alone carry limited information about phase:** macro-F1 of 0.46
  against a chance level of 0.25.
- **Self-reports add about twice as much as hormones when combined with wearables**
  (ΔF1 = +0.11 vs. +0.06), and are cheaper to collect.
- **The best model used hormones and self-reports without wearables** (XGBoost,
  macro-F1 = 0.63, AUROC = 0.86).
- **Performance is limited mainly by the number of participants.** Validation
  performance was still increasing at the largest training cohort tested, and the
  wearables-only models overfit to individual participants.

## Background

### Why cycle phase matters

The menstrual cycle is commonly divided into four phases. Each is defined by a
characteristic pattern of estradiol, progesterone, and luteinizing hormone (LH):

- **Menstrual:** bleeding, with low hormone levels.
- **Follicular:** estradiol rises as a follicle matures.
- **Fertility:** the window around ovulation, which an LH surge triggers.
- **Luteal:** progesterone dominates after ovulation.

Phase affects sleep, thermoregulation, cardiovascular function, mood, and symptom
burden. As a result, it is relevant to fertility planning and personal health
monitoring. It is also a confounder in clinical and physiological research, where it
often goes unmeasured.

The reference methods for determining phase are serial hormone measurement and
ultrasound. Both are accurate, but neither is practical for continuous use outside a
study. Most consumer apps instead estimate phase by counting days since the last
period and assuming a fixed cycle template. Cycle length and phase durations vary both
between people and from one cycle to the next within the same person, so these
calendar estimates become less reliable the further they are from the last period.

### What wearables could offer

Many of the physiological changes across the cycle are measured continuously by
consumer wearables:

- **Temperature:** basal body temperature rises after ovulation.
- **Heart rate:** resting heart rate tends to be higher in the luteal phase.
- **Heart rate variability (HRV):** HRV and sleep architecture change across phases.

If these signals were enough to identify phase, monitoring could be continuous and
almost cost-free. In practice, consumer data are noisy: wear time is inconsistent,
nights are missing, devices differ, and baselines vary widely between individuals. The
cycle-related changes are also small relative to those other sources of variation.

### Research question

> **How much does classification of cycle phase improve when hormonal and/or self-reported
> measures are added to wearable data, and how does each gain compare with the cost of
> collecting that data?**

Framed this way, the question is about decisions rather than only about accuracy. Each
data source has a different cost to collect, and the useful result is the marginal
value of each source, not the model with the highest score.

### Dataset

[mcPHASES](https://doi.org/10.13026/zx6a-2c81)<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> provides
data collected simultaneously from three sources in the same participants. The daily
variables selected from each source were:

| Source           | Variables                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Burden of collection           |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| **Wearables**    | **Heart:** heart rate, resting heart rate, HRV (RMSSD, high-frequency power, low-frequency power)<br />**Sleep:** total sleep time, minutes of deep, light, and REM sleep, sleep efficiency<br />**Activity:** minutes of light, moderate, and high activity, step count<br />**Body:** skin temperature, temperature deviation from baseline, respiratory rate, estimated VO₂ max | Passive (Fitbit)               |
| **Hormones**     | estrogen<br />luteinizing hormone (LH)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             | Requires daily assays          |
| **Self-reports** | **Menstrual:** flow volume (8-point scale), cramps, sore breasts<br />**Digestive:** appetite, food cravings, indigestion, bloating<br />**General:** headaches, fatigue, sleep issues, mood swings, stress, exercise level                                                                                                                                                        | Daily entry by the participant |

Each symptom was rated on a 6-point scale from "Not at all" to "Very High".

The dataset provides a phase label for each day, which served as the ground truth for
classification.

## Methods

### Preprocessing

The wearable data comes as about ten separate exports, each recorded at a different
resolution: minute-level heart rate, sleep sessions, and step counts per interval.
`src/dataset.py` harmonizes them into one row per participant-day and merges them with
the hormone and self-report tables.

- **Duplicate records:** where a participant had duplicate records for the same day,
  the pipeline kept the better-sampled one. For example, it chose the temperature
  record with the most samples and the longest main sleep session, rather than an
  arbitrary record.
- **Non-physiological values:** readings such as zero hormone concentrations or
  negative HRV were set to missing instead of removing the whole day. This keeps the
  day's other valid measureme3nts.
- **Computed variables:**
  - **Wear time:** minutes per day that the device was worn, used later as a QC
    criterion. Heart rate was resampled into 5-minute bins, and wear time was estimated
    as the number of bins containing a heart-rate reading, multiplied by 5.
  - **Cycle boundaries:** defined as each return to the Menstrual phase. This gives
    every day a cycle index and a flag for whether its cycle is complete.

### Quality control

`src/qc.py` runs as a separate stage of the pipeline, in two parts:

- **Exclusion criteria:**
  - days with less than 10 hours of wear
  - participants with fewer than 30 valid days
- **Diagnostic checks, logged for review rather than applied automatically:**
  - columns with more than 75% missing values
  - medians outside physiological reference ranges
  - pairs of features with |r| ≥ 0.9

QC excluded only 4 of 3,698 participant-days
and no participants, leaving **42 participants and 3,694 participant-days**.

### Feature engineering

- **Within-participant normalization.** Wearable and hormone features were z-scored
  separately for each participant. As a result, the model sees deviations from each
  person's own baseline rather than differences between people. This matters because
  differences between people in, for example, resting heart rate are typically larger
  than the changes across a single cycle.
- **Temporal context.** Rolling means and standard deviations over 7 and 14 days were
  computed for every source. Phase changes slowly, while single-day measurements are
  noisy.
- **Derived physiological ratios:** the HRV low-frequency/high-frequency ratio and the
  estrogen/LH ratio.
- **Encoding of symptoms:** each symptom scale was converted to an ordinal number.
- **Cyclical position.** Period onset was identified from self-reported flow, defined
  as consecutive days of moderate or heavier flow. The number of days since onset was
  then encoded as sine and cosine terms, so that the end of one cycle sits next to the
  start of the next. These features use only information a user would record
  themselves. Because they are closely related to how the phase labels are defined,
  they are discussed again under [Limitations](#limitations).

Training combined the per-source feature tables into all **seven subsets of sources**.
Comparing these subsets gives the marginal contribution of each source.

### Modeling and evaluation

- **Generalization to new individuals.** Participants were split 30 / 6 / 6 into
  training, validation, and test sets. No participant contributed days to more than
  one set. Days from the same person are strongly correlated, so a random split by day
  would let a model score well by recognizing individuals rather than phase, and would
  overestimate performance. A split by participant tests the scenario that matters in
  practice: a new user.
- **Models:**
  - multinomial logistic regression, a linear, interpretable baseline
  - gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost), which can model non-linear relationships and
    interactions
- **Tuning:** hyperparameters were tuned with Optuna (50 trials for each combination
  of model and source subset) to maximize macro-F1 on the validation set. The test set
  was evaluated once.
- **Metrics:** accuracy, macro-F1, macro-recall, and one-vs-rest AUROC. Macro-F1 is the
  primary metric because it weights the four phases equally regardless of how often
  each occurs.

### Interpretability

Feature attributions use SHAP values, which split each prediction into the
contribution of each feature. The `shap` library's dependency on `numba` does not
support this project's Python version, so I implemented exact attributions directly:

- **XGBoost:** Tree SHAP, through the model's native `pred_contribs` output.
- **Logistic regression:** the closed-form linear SHAP solution,
  φᵢ = βᵢ · (xᵢ − E[xᵢ]).

## Results

### Contribution of each data source

<img
  src="/figures/project-6/eval_test.png"
  alt="Macro-F1 across all seven source combinations"
  class="mx-auto w-full max-w-sm"
/>

The best model for each source subset, on held-out participants:

| Sources                             | Best model | Macro-F1  | ΔF1 vs. wearables | AUROC |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------- | --------- | ----------------- | ----- |
| Wearables _(reference)_             | LR         | 0.463     | —                 | 0.738 |
| Wearables + hormones                | XGB        | 0.519     | +0.056            | 0.782 |
| Wearables + self-reports            | XGB        | 0.574     | +0.111            | 0.839 |
| Wearables + hormones + self-reports | XGB        | 0.621     | +0.158            | 0.871 |
| **Hormones + self-reports**         | **XGB**    | **0.630** | **+0.167**        | 0.863 |

1. **Wearables carry information about phase, but not enough to use on their own.**
   Wearable-only models perform clearly above chance, but they misclassify about half
   of all days.
2. **Self-reports are the most valuable source to add to wearables.** Their gain is
   about twice that of hormones, and they require no laboratory assays.
3. **Once hormones and self-reports are included, wearables add nothing detectable.**
   The model without wearables (0.630) and the model with all three sources (0.621)
   differ by less than can be resolved with a test set of 6 participants. The
   three-source model has a marginally higher AUROC (0.871 vs. 0.863), so wearables may
   slightly improve how confidently the model ranks phases.

### Pattern of errors

<img
  src="/figures/project-6/fitbit_hormones_selfreports_confusion.png"
  alt="Confusion matrix, all three sources"
  class="mx-auto w-full max-w-lg"
/>

Errors are concentrated between phases that are adjacent in time:

- **Menstrual and Luteal**, which meet where one cycle ends and the next begins.
- **Follicular and Fertility.**

Phases on opposite sides of the cycle, such as Menstrual and Fertility, are rarely
confused. This error structure is what one would expect if the model has learned the
cycle's temporal organization. Most of the remaining error comes from imprecise phase
boundaries rather than from wrong predictions about where in the cycle a day falls.

### Feature attributions

<img
  src="/figures/project-6/shap_summary_by_class.png"
  alt="SHAP values by class"
  class="mx-auto w-full max-w-full"
/>

- **Hormonal features** (LH, estrogen, and their rolling means) contribute most to the
  Follicular, Fertility, and Luteal predictions. This agrees with the known
  physiology: the rise in estradiol before ovulation and the LH surge that marks the
  fertile window.
- **Wearable features** (resting heart rate, sleep composition, temperature) contribute
  mainly to the Menstrual prediction, and their attributions are smaller.
- **Cycle position and flow volume have the largest attributions overall.** This is
  expected, but it limits how far the self-report results can be interpreted (see
  [Limitations](#limitations)).

### Generalization diagnostics

<img
  src="/figures/project-6/eval_gap.png"
  alt="Train–test F1 gap by source subset and model"
  class="mx-auto w-full max-w-sm"
/>

- **The wearable-only XGBoost model has the largest gap between training and test
  performance** (ΔF1 ≈ 0.47), meaning it overfits to the training participants. This
  is the only source subset where logistic regression outperforms XGBoost. That
  pattern is consistent with a weak signal relative to the noise, where a more flexible
  model fits participant-specific noise.
- **Models that include hormones and self-reports generalize best**, with gaps of about
  0.15 to 0.20.

<img
  src="/figures/project-6/learning_curve_fitbit_hormones_selfreports_xgboost.png"
  alt="Learning curve, all three sources, XGBoost"
  class="mx-auto w-full max-w-lg"
/>

Validation macro-F1 rises from 0.51 with 5 training participants to 0.63 with 30, and
the curve has not leveled off. **Performance is currently limited by the number of
participants rather than by model capacity.** Recruiting more participants is likely to
improve results more than further model tuning would, which is directly relevant to
planning future data collection.

## Limitations

- **Statistical power.** The test set contains 6 participants. The ranking of source
  subsets is broadly consistent across both model families, but the individual metric values
  have wide confidence intervals, which have not yet been estimated.
- **Features closely related to the label definition.** Flow volume is close to the
  definition of the Menstrual phase, and the cycle-position features are counted from
  flow-based period onset. Both are realistically available in a tracking app. Even
  so, part of the self-report advantage probably reflects tracking the time since the
  last period rather than information from symptoms. The wearable-only and
  hormone-only results do not include these features and are not affected.
- **Normalization uses information from later days.** Each participant's z-score
  parameters are computed from their entire recording, including days after the one
  being classified. A deployed system would need a baseline that only uses past data,
  which would probably reduce performance somewhat.
- **Reproducibility.** The Optuna sampler is not seeded, so metrics vary slightly
  between runs. The participant split is fixed.
- **Compliance outside a study.** Hormone assays and symptom diaries depend on
  participants testing and logging every day. In the study that collected this
  dataset<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>, participants were paid for each day of complete data collection. Some participants said they would only use the hormone
  test again if trying to conceive, and several said that before the study they had
  only logged their period dates, not symptoms. Everyday users without these
  incentives are likely to test and log less consistently. Wearables collect data
  passively and only need to be worn and charged, so they are much less affected. The
  gains from hormones and self-reports may therefore be smaller in real-world use than
  they are here.

## Future work

1. **Ablation of flow and cycle-position features.** This would measure how much
   information the symptom ratings carry on their own.
2. **Expanding-window normalization.** Each day would be normalized using only data
   available up to that day, as in deployment.
3. **Cross-validation that holds out whole participants**, in place of a single split.
   This would provide confidence intervals and allow formal comparison between models.
4. **Temporal models.** Phases occur in a fixed order, so a hidden Markov model or a
   sequence model could enforce valid transitions. This would directly address the
   errors between adjacent phases.

## Implementation

```
src/
├── dataset.py        raw exports → participant-day table
├── qc.py             exclusion criteria and diagnostic checks
├── features.py       normalization, rolling statistics, encodings
├── plots.py          result figures
└── modeling/
    ├── train.py        participant-level split, metrics, exact SHAP
    ├── classifiers.py  model pipelines and Optuna search
    ├── diagnostics.py  generalization gap, learning and validation curves
    └── predict.py      inference
```

- **Stages:** each stage is a command-line step that reads from one data directory and
  writes to the next (`raw/` → `interim/` → `processed/`). Any stage can be re-run on
  its own.
- **Run log:** every training run is appended to `models/results/runs.jsonl` with its
  parameters and metrics, so each experiment can be traced back to its configuration.
- **Diagnostics:** the generalization diagnostics reuse the tuned hyperparameters and
  do not repeat the search.
- **Tooling:** `loguru` for logging, `ruff` for linting and formatting, and `pipenv`
  for managing the environment.
